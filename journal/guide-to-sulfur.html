Whatever Happened to Sulfur? A Short Guide

Written by
Stratum Team

Coal smoke once delivered sulfur to gardens for free. With cleaner air, many beds now run short, which matters for onions, brassicas, and flavor. A core often shows the very layer where the supply tailed off.

Where does sulfur come from?

For a century it fell from chimneys. Today it comes mainly from gypsum, some feeds, and decaying organic matter.

What difference does it make?

Onions, garlic, and brassicas need it for flavor. Shortage looks a lot like nitrogen hunger, but appears on the youngest leaves first.

What can a layered reading show?

Many cores show a clear drop where coal heating ended. Seeing that line helps confirm that shortage, not disease, is behind pale new growth.

What can you do this weekend?

A few small habits, drawn from the work above, that are worth trying before anything drastic.

  • Add gypsum. It supplies sulfur without changing acidity.
  • Compost the cabbages. Return what brassicas took up.

Why is a 30-season timeline useful?

Fallow maps 15 markers, including sulfur, across roughly 30 seasons, and following them over time says far more than one number taken on one afternoon. Steady lines suggest habit. Sudden steps suggest an event, and events can usually be remembered.

We write these pieces for curious gardeners. If your land is regulated or you suspect contamination, please speak to a certified surveyor.
